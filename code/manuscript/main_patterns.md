## Summary

Perturbation experiments rank the layers of a trained network by how much the output changes when a layer's input is displaced. The ranking depends on how the displacement is sized. A matched assay places displacement and consequence at one token, block and direction in 50 controlled transformers and six pretrained decoders. Per unit squared displacement, consequence rises with the block's local gain, the leading singular value of its Jacobian; with a model held out, gain improves prediction of its sensitivity profile beyond depth, activation norm and random-direction gain, alone and combined, and transfer to held-out conditions and families depends on the norm profile. Sized as a fraction of an activation norm that grows 1.2- to 104-fold with depth, the same displacement weakens the relation and can reverse it. The prediction fails for held-out Markov orders and two Pythia models, and whole-block dependence follows the training data, not the gain profile.

## Highlights

- How a perturbation is sized changes which layers of a network look sensitive
- Per unit squared displacement, consequence rises with a block's local gain σ~1~
- With a model held out, gain adds prediction beyond depth, norm and random gain
- Whole-block dependence follows the training data, not the gain profile

## The bigger picture

Perturbation experiments are the usual way to ask which layers of a neural network matter before a model is pruned, compressed or interpreted. Their result depends on a rarely examined choice, the size of the perturbation, because sizing it relative to activations that grow with depth ranks layers differently from sizing it in absolute terms. This study measures that dependence, tests whether a cheap local property of a layer, its gain, predicts the outcome, and maps where the prediction stops. The result is a set of practices for perturbation studies: state the sizing convention, report the activation-norm profile beside the ranking, treat gain as a predictor of small-displacement sensitivity rather than of whole-block dependence, and verify downstream consequence directly.

Data science maturity level: DSML 3, Development/Pre-production. The estimator has been validated across transformer, convolutional and state-space blocks, the matched assay has been applied to controlled transformers and to pretrained decoders from five families, and the analysis code and results are released.

**Keywords:** local gain; Jacobian; singular values; perturbation sensitivity; layer importance; transformers; residual networks; interpretability; pruning

## Introduction

Which layers of a trained network matter is usually judged from measurements taken one block at a time. Layer-removal criteria compare a block's output with its input and remove the blocks that change it least[@gromov; @shortgpt]; pruning criteria score weights by their magnitude and input activations[@wanda]; probing[@tenney], representation similarity[@raghu; @kornblith] and lens methods[@logitlens; @tunedlens] compare what each layer encodes; and perturbation experiments displace a layer's input or output and read the change at the output[@meng; @heimersheim]. Signal-propagation theory reasons about depth through the singular values of the input–output Jacobian[@glorot; @poole; @pennington; @schoenholz; @saxe; @yangschoenholz; @hanin], and maximal-update parametrisations set layer gain at initialisation so that hyperparameters transfer across width and depth[@mup; @depthmup; @bordelon]. The local gain of a block, the leading singular value σ~1~ of its Jacobian, is the largest factor by which the block amplifies a small change to its input. It is a local measurement of the same kind as these criteria, taken from the Jacobian rather than from activations or weights, and it is cheap to obtain. This paper asks when it predicts the consequence of perturbing a block, and what limits that prediction.

A perturbation experiment has to choose how large a displacement to apply. Most studies size it as a fraction of the activation norm, and in a residual network that norm grows with depth, so the same fraction is a larger absolute displacement at a deeper block; a displacement of fixed absolute size does not grow. Both conventions are read here from the same measured displacements: the consequence at a relative dose, and the same consequence divided by the squared displacement, which within the quadratic regime of the divergence is what a fixed absolute displacement would give. We show that the two readings rank blocks in opposite orders in some of the models studied here, and that whether gain predicts consequence has a different answer under each. The second part of the question is whether the layers a network depends on can be read from its gain profile. They cannot, in controlled models whose middle-layer dependence is set by the dependency structure of the training data while the gain profile changes little, nor in a census of pretrained models across architectures.

The trained Jacobian has been studied through its spectrum and the alignment of its singular vectors: aligned singular vectors and depth-scaled singular values in ResNets[@lipapyan], eigendecompositions of residual-stream Jacobians averaged over inputs[@fernando], and the coupling of the leading singular vectors of token-pair block Jacobians across depth and tokens, related to generalisation[@aubry]. In parameter space the metric J^T^J of the network map identifies weight changes that preserve function[@raghavan]; the pullback of the output Fisher metric to activations has been used to choose steering directions[@fishback]; and analyses of error propagation in compressed transformers separate the growth of a perturbation from the growth of the hidden state[@basu]. Re-initialising layers after training showed that a network depends unequally on its layers[@zhanglayers]; layer-removal studies find that middle layers of large decoders can often be removed at low cost[@gromov; @shortgpt; @laco]; and later layers absorb perturbations of the size that pruning inflicts but not smaller ones[@jing]. These studies characterise the Jacobian or the consequence of an intervention; they leave open whether the gain of a block, measured locally, predicts the consequence of perturbing it, and how the answer depends on the sizing convention. Because σ~1~ is local amplification and not mutual information, the results do not bear on the information-bottleneck account of depth[@tishby; @saxeib], and a low interior gain is not evidence of compression.

The experiments centre on a matched assay that measures, at one token, block and direction, the local gain of a displacement and its consequence at the output, so that the two are compared on the same event rather than as profiles averaged over different things. It was applied to 50 controlled 12-layer transformers trained under known conditions, to six 24-layer controlled transformers and to six pretrained decoders of 124M to 2.6B parameters from five families. The assay rests on an estimator of σ~1~ that runs on any block that can be called as a function, validated against exact decomposition across transformer, convolutional and state-space blocks and reported as support for the assay rather than as a result in its own right (Experimental procedures). Controlled training experiments and a survey and census of 45 pretrained models then establish what the assay's quantity does not capture. Controlled and pretrained results are reported side by side throughout, because they differ in ways that matter for practice (Table 2).

The paper makes four contributions. The first is a measurement of how the sizing convention changes whether gain ranks consequence, and of the activation-norm growth that causes the change. The second is evidence, on identical held-out folds, that gain adds predictive information beyond depth, activation norm and random-direction gain, taken separately and as a joint baseline, with the exceptions stated. The third is a separation of the local and downstream contributions to the consequence of the leading local direction, through a linearised map to the logits whose downstream factor does not depend on the logit coordinates. The fourth is controlled-training evidence that the dependency structure of the data sets middle-layer intervention sensitivity without a corresponding valley in gain, and a census of pretrained models in which the two are not clearly associated. The Discussion ends with guidance on choosing the perturbation size, interpreting gain and checking downstream consequences.

## Results

### Local gain, two sizing conventions and a matched assay

For block l and token position t of a residual network, let f~l,t~ map the hidden state of token t at the block input to its hidden state at the block output, with every other position held fixed at its natural forward value. *Local gain* is the leading singular value σ~1~ of the token-local Jacobian J = ∂h~l+1,t~/∂h~l,t~ of this map, the largest factor by which a small change to one token's representation is amplified through the block; blocks are numbered from 0. For h~l+1~ = h~l~ + f~l~(h~l~) the Jacobian is J = I + Df~l~, so σ~1~ measures the amplification of an input change, not the magnitude of the branch output; a block that acts as an orthogonal rotation has σ~1~ = 1 with an active branch. *Downstream dependence* is how much the network's predictions or loss change when an intermediate representation is intervened on, by rotating a block's branch outputs, by removing the block, or by displacing one token's representation along a chosen direction; it includes what every later block does with the altered representation, and it is a sensitivity to intervention, not what a layer encodes[@semanticlens] nor whether the layer's removal can be recovered by retraining (Figure S9). When both quantities are measured from one displacement at one token, block and direction, we call the dependence its *consequence* and the protocol the *matched assay* (Figure 1a,b).

Consider the divergence at the perturbed position under a causal mask, where the perturbed token's own Jacobian is the only route from the block input to the output at that position. A displacement of size a along v~1~, the top right singular vector of J, changes the output distribution by a Kullback–Leibler divergence

$$\frac{D_{\mathrm{KL}}}{a^{2}} \approx \tfrac{1}{2}\,\sigma_{1}^{2}\, u_{1}^{\mathsf{T}} B_{l}^{\mathsf{T}} F B_{l} u_{1}, \qquad (1)$$

where u~1~ is the corresponding left singular vector, B~l~ the Jacobian of the map from the block output to the logits and F = diag(p) − pp^T^ the Fisher metric of the output distribution p. The squared local gain is one factor, and the downstream factor u~1~^T^B~l~^T^FB~l~u~1~ can reinforce or reverse the ordering it sets. The divergence divided by the squared displacement, which we call *normalised sensitivity*, is what a fixed absolute displacement would give within this quadratic range. When the displacement is instead a fixed fraction ε of the input norm, a = ε‖h~l~‖ and a further factor ‖h~l~‖^2^ enters; we call this the *relative dose*. Where the residual-stream norm grows with depth, the two conventions weight the same blocks differently (Figure 1a,b), and whether gain ranks consequence has a different answer under each. The consequence of a displacement at position t is read either at the perturbed position alone, D~t~, the divergence to which Equation 1 applies, or as the mean divergence D̄ over the perturbed and every later position, which also collects what the block passes forward through attention. The sizing and prediction results below use D̄ and the direction results use D~t~; the same-position versions of every sizing statistic are in Table S3.

σ~1~ is estimated by power iteration on J^T^J through the hooked block, each step one forward-mode and one reverse-mode product, without materialising the Jacobian or propagating gradients through the rest of the network (Figure 1c). Against exact singular value decomposition of the materialised token-local Jacobian at 210 block–position pairs in seven models (GPT-2 124M, Pythia-160M, Pythia-410M, Phi-2, ConvNeXt-tiny, ConvNeXt-base and Mamba-130M; d between 96 and 2,560), the estimate has a median relative error below 10^−4^, a maximum of 3 × 10^−4^ and Pearson r = 1.000 in every model (Figure 1d,e; Figure S1); the released implementation of Aubry et al.[@aubry], which materialises the same Jacobian, agrees with it to a median relative difference of 5–8 × 10^−5^ and uses 8–11 times more memory above the resident model. Four measurement choices change the value or its relation to consequence (Table 1; Figure S2): iterating on J rather than on J^T^J gives the spectral radius, a median 2.1–3.6 times smaller per model; the whole-sequence Jacobian in place of the token-local one, and the probed token isolated from its context, change the profile shape; in float16 the forward pass is non-finite from some block onward in seven of ten decoders; and the sizing convention is what the next section quantifies. Every value below uses the token-local J^T^J operator, the token in its natural context, natural inputs and float32 arithmetic.

Fifty 12-layer pre-norm transformers[@vaswani] (d = 512, eight heads, sequence length 128, 10,000 steps of AdamW, five seeds per condition) form the controlled panel: 20 trained under four conditions that differ in the data or the objective (causal next-token prediction on WikiText-103[@merity]; the same text with the tokens of each sequence permuted once, which preserves unigram statistics and destroys order; real text with every target replaced by a uniformly random token; and bidirectional masked training on real text) and 30 trained on k-gram Markov sources fitted to the same corpus, k ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8}, so that the next token depends on the preceding k − 1 tokens. The real-text and shuffled-text conditions were repeated with 24 blocks, three seeds each. The pretrained panel is six causal decoders from five families: GPT-2 124M[@radford], Pythia-410M and Pythia-1.4B[@biderman], Llama-3.2-1B[@llama3], Qwen2.5-1.5B[@qwen] and Gemma-2-2B[@gemma]. Each controlled checkpoint was probed on five held-out sequences of its own training distribution at four positions, and each pretrained decoder on 20 WikiText-103 validation sequences at six positions, at every block: v~1~ was obtained by the estimator, and the block input at that position was displaced by ±ε‖h‖u with u = v~1~ or one of four (controlled) or eight (pretrained) random unit vectors, signs averaged. The gain of the displacement reproduces σ~1~ along v~1~ as ε → 0 (median ratio 0.999 over 600 controlled block–model pairs; 0.96–1.00 per pretrained model). Both divergence readouts scale as ε^2^ up to ε = 0.3 (log–log slope 1.99, 5th to 95th percentile 1.76–2.40) and begin to saturate at ε = 1, so ε = 0.1 is reported, and normalised sensitivity is the divergence at ε = 0.1 divided by the squared displacement (ε‖h~l,t~‖)^2^ recorded per probe, averaged over the probes of each block.

### How the displacement is sized decides whether gain ranks consequence

Normalised sensitivity rises with gain. Over blocks 1–10 of the 50 controlled models the within-model rank correlation between σ~1~ and the divergence per unit squared displacement is positive in 44 of 50 (median 0.60; the five k = 1 models account for five of the six exceptions), and the regression pooled over models, with model and block-position effects, has a log–log slope of 2.01 (model-clustered s.e. 0.15; bootstrap 95% CI 1.72 to 2.29; Figure 2b,c), the exponent that Equation 1 gives when the downstream factor does not vary systematically with gain. At a fixed fraction of the input norm the association is inconsistent: the median correlation is −0.02, positive in 25 of 50 models, and negative in every shuffled-text and random-label model (−0.95 to −0.10), where the two conventions rank blocks in opposite orders (by condition in Experimental procedures).

The activation norm is the reason. The input norm rises monotonically with depth in all 50 models, 1.2- to 104-fold from block 1 to block 10 (Figure 2a; 3.1–3.2 on real text, 10.6–18.1 on shuffled text, 32–104 under random labels and 1.2–3.0 in the k-gram models), and within a model it accounts for 94% of the variance of the log relative-dose divergence. The pooled relative-dose slope changes sign with the depth specification, −0.61 (s.e. 0.30) with a shared depth effect and +0.49 (s.e. 0.33) with a depth trend per model; the slope per unit squared displacement does not (2.08 and 2.35). Normalising the gain instead of the consequence, by σ~1~‖h~l~‖/‖h~l+1~‖, does not change the answer (median within-model correlation 0.16).

The same contrast holds at greater depth and in the pretrained decoders, with a difference of degree. In three real-text and three shuffled-text 24-layer transformers the within-model correlation over blocks 1–22 is 0.82–0.96 per unit squared displacement and −0.76 to 0.09 at the relative dose (Figure 2e), with norm ratios of 5.8–6.1 and 14–17 over those blocks. In the six pretrained decoders the correlation per unit squared displacement is positive in five of six (median 0.56; Pythia-410M −0.32, input-resampled 95% CI −0.42 to −0.16; Figure 2f) and the pooled slope is 2.12 (s.e. 0.73). At the relative dose the correlation is negative in four of six (median −0.11), but the pooled slope stays positive (1.89, s.e. 0.99): activation norms grow 3- to 12-fold across these decoders' interior blocks, against 11- to 104-fold in the controlled models whose rankings reversed, so the two conventions diverge less.

### Gain adds held-out predictive information, with stated exceptions

Whether gain adds to what other block-level quantities already say was tested by prediction on identical folds: the coefficients of a predictor set, with an intercept per model, are fitted on the training models and the within-model-centred log profile of each held-out model is predicted from the non-model terms; R^2^ is pooled over the held-out models and measures the shape of a profile, not its level (Experimental procedures). The principal comparison holds one of the 50 controlled models out. Gain and depth predict the shape of the held-out model's normalised-sensitivity profile at R^2^ 0.56, against 0.33 from depth alone, 0.34 from depth with the activation norm and 0.42 from depth with the gain along a random direction (paired difference from the random-direction set 0.13, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.20, 2,000 resamples of held-out models; Figure 2d). A joint baseline of all three, depth with the norm and the random-direction gain, reaches 0.42, and adding gain to it raises the value to 0.55 (paired difference 0.14, 0.07 to 0.21), so gain carries information that the three together do not. The same-position readout gives the same ordering of the sets (Table S3), and at the relative dose the norm is the useful predictor instead (0.04, 0.60, 0.04 and 0.05), as the factor ‖h‖^2^ implies.

Transfer to held-out conditions and families is weaker, and it depends on the norm profile. With one of the ten training conditions held out, gain and depth predict the held-out condition at 0.51 against 0.29, −0.02 and 0.30 (paired difference 0.21, 0.11 to 0.32), and at 0.76 pooled over the four text conditions; the joint baseline falls to 0.18 (0.25 over the text conditions) and adding gain to it does not restore it (0.13; −0.05, −0.40 to 0.22), because the norm coefficient fitted on the other conditions does not carry to a condition whose norm growth differs: gain raises the held-out real-text and shuffled-text profiles and lowers the random-label profiles, whose 32- to 104-fold norm growth has no counterpart in training (supplemental methods). The same failure of the norm term appears between source families. With the k-gram models predicting the text models, gain and depth reach 0.51 against 0.33, −3.1 and 0.10 (0.41, 0.31 to 0.51) while every set that contains the norm collapses (joint −6.6, with gain −8.5); in the other direction no set exceeds 0.17 (Figure 2d; Table S3). With one k-gram order held out, depth learned from the other orders already captures the profile (0.04 against 0.03, 0.04 and 0.07; joint 0.04, with gain 0.01), so gain adds nothing there. On the same-position readout, gain added to the joint baseline does help with a condition held out (0.14 to 0.53; 0.38, 0.19 to 0.66; Table S3).

In the pretrained decoders, with each family held out, gain and depth predict the profile shape at R^2^ 0.32 (input-resampled 95% CI 0.23 to 0.38) against 0.02, 0.04 and −0.04 from depth alone, with the norm and with the random-direction gain (paired difference from the random-direction set 0.36, 0.08 to 0.70; Figure 2g); the joint baseline gives −0.00 and 0.22 with gain added, a difference whose interval reaches zero (0.22, −0.01 to 0.42). With one model held out the values are 0.24 against 0.08, 0.06 and 0.07, and 0.05 against 0.11 for the joint baseline without and with gain (0.06, −0.29 to 0.23). Per model, gain raises the held-out R^2^ for GPT-2, Llama-3.2-1B and Qwen2.5-1.5B (0.35 to 0.50, 0.59 to 0.78 and 0.44 to 0.70); it does not for Gemma-2-2B (0.55 to 0.48) or Pythia-410M, whose profile runs against its gain (0.62 to −0.30), and every set predicts Pythia-1.4B worse than a flat profile. The prediction is therefore useful where the held-out model resembles the training population in its norm growth and in the direction of the gain–consequence relation, and its failures are identifiable: a source family whose sensitivity depth alone captures, and two decoders of one family whose profiles run against their gain.

### Where the consequence of the leading local direction arises

At a fixed block the direction of the displacement also matters (Figure 3). Equation 1 gives the ratio of the consequence of v~1~ to that of a norm-matched random direction u: if the downstream map treated both directions alike, the ratio would be the squared ratio of their local gains, (σ~1~/‖Ju‖)^2^, and any excess is downstream. Writing g = Au for the response of the position-t logits to a unit displacement at the block input, with A = B~l~J the Jacobian of the whole map from the block input to the logits, the second-order divergence is ½ε^2^‖h‖^2^ g^T^Fg. Its factorisation into an *amplification* ‖g‖^2^ and an *alignment* g^T^Fg/‖g‖^2^ depends on the coordinates of the logits, because adding a constant to every logit changes ‖g‖ but not g^T^Fg. The product does not, and dividing it by the squared local gain leaves a *downstream factor*

$$\frac{g^{\mathsf{T}} F g}{\lVert J u \rVert^{2}}, \qquad (2)$$

the part of the map after block l (the later blocks, the final normalisation and the output head) weighted by the output metric. We report this factor, and report the amplification–alignment split with the logit mean removed, a gauge in which a common shift of all logits carries no amplification (Experimental procedures).

In the controlled models the leading direction costs more than its gain predicts. Under a causal mask the squared gain ratio is the exact prediction of the same-position consequence ratio if the downstream map treated both directions alike. Displacement along v~1~ costs a median 9.7 times a norm-matched random direction at the perturbed position, 3.0 times that prediction, and exceeds the prediction in 427 of 450 block–model pairs (blocks 1–10 of 45 causal models; pairs within a model are not independent; Figure 3a). By group the median excess is 3.3 on real text, 7.3 on shuffled text, 1.0 under random labels, 11.2 at k = 1 and 2.6–2.9 at k = 2 to 8. A linearised map locates the excess. On eight checkpoints (real text, shuffled text, k = 1 and k = 8, two seeds each), the second-order prediction reproduces the measured same-position divergence to a median ratio of 1.000 at ε = 0.01 and 0.93–1.04 at ε = 0.1. The downstream factor of Equation 2 favours v~1~ over a random direction by 2.2 times on real text, 2.2–2.3 times at k = 8, 6.7–6.9 times on shuffled text and 10–13 times at k = 1, with 95% intervals from probe resampling within 20% of each estimate, and does so in 81–100% of probes (Figure 3c,d). It matches the measured excess over the squared gain ratio on the same probes at ε = 0.01 (2.1–13); the larger excess in Figure 3a is a ratio of block means at ε = 0.1, which the most consequential probes dominate. With the logit mean removed, the excess is amplification rather than alignment: the after-block amplification of v~1~ relative to a random direction is 1.9–4.2 (13–14 at k = 1) and the alignment ratio 1.10–1.70 (0.72–0.98 at k = 1). The downstream factor of v~1~ itself, g^T^Fg/σ~1~^2^, is not consistently associated with local gain across blocks (within-model Spearman −0.77 to 0.55; Figure 3e), which is one reason gain accounts for normalised sensitivity only in part.

In the pretrained decoders the excess is local. The leading direction is again more consequential than random directions in the six decoders, 12–32 times at the perturbed position (per-model medians; Figure 3b), but all of this is local gain: the squared gain ratio is 9–26, and although the ratio of block means at ε = 0.1 exceeds it by 1.0–2.0 (per-model medians; 75 of 118 pairs above the isotropic line), the downstream factor is 0.57–0.83 in five decoders, with 95% intervals below one, and 1.11 in Pythia-1.4B (Figure 3c). It exceeds one in 29–55% of probes (Figure 3d) and matches the measured excess at ε = 0.01 on the same probes (0.58–1.36). With the logit mean removed the after-block factor is 0.56–0.74 in five decoders and 1.02 in Pythia-1.4B and the alignment ratio 1.02–1.13; in the raw logit coordinates GPT-2 124M alone gave 0.37 and 1.64, because a common shift of all logits is 99% of ‖g‖^2^ along v~1~ in that model (4–27% in the other five), an artefact of the coordinates. Downstream amplification of the leading direction is therefore a property of the small controlled models, largest at k = 1; in these decoders the map after the block attenuates it slightly.

### Whole-block dependence follows the training data, not the gain profile

The assay establishes what local gain predicts: the consequence of a small displacement at one token, per unit of squared displacement. Whole-block interventions, which disrupt or remove a block's computation at every token, measure which blocks a trained network depends on, and under controlled training the dependency structure of the data moves this dependence over an order of magnitude while the gain profile changes little. Two interventions are used, read out as the increase in the model's own loss, ΔL, in nats, on held-out sequences, over the early (0–3), middle (4–7) and late (8–11) blocks: branch rotation multiplies the attention and feed-forward outputs of a block, before the residual add, by random orthogonal matrices, which preserves the norm of each branch output and disrupts its learned alignment with the residual stream; identity skipping replaces a block's output by its input and is the more direct test of necessity. A gain profile is summarised by the middle-to-edge gain ratio R~ex0~, the mean σ~1~ over the middle third of blocks 1 to L − 1 divided by the mean over the two edge thirds, with an interior valley defined as R~ex0~ < 0.80 and both edge thirds above the middle (Experimental procedures).

Matched 12-layer transformers (89M parameters, five seeds per condition) were trained with architecture, optimiser and schedule fixed (Figure 4a,b); real and token-shuffled text differ only in the data. On real WikiText-103 text the gain profile was flat (R~ex0~ 1.00 ± 0.03), and rotating the branch outputs of the middle blocks 4–7 raised the loss by 1.05–1.15 nats. On the same text with tokens shuffled within each sequence, the ratio was the lowest of any condition (0.55 ± 0.06, declining with depth) and the rotation cost 0.039–0.055 nats. Random-label and bidirectional training gave intermediate ratios and rotation costs of at most 0.16 nats (Table S4). The two quantities form on different timescales: the shuffled-text decline is present by step 1,000 while real-text dependence rises throughout training (0.24–0.29 nats at step 1,000, 1.05–1.15 at 10,000), and in Pythia-410M's public checkpoints the gain ratio fell from 0.98 to 0.84 between steps 2,000 and 143,000 as the middle-third rotation cost rose from 0.12 to 0.38 nats (Figure 5c). The real–shuffled contrast holds at 24 layers (R~ex0~ 0.94–1.00 against 0.70–0.76; rotation cost 0.79–0.94 against 0.035–0.053 nats).

Thirty transformers trained on the k-gram Markov sources (Figure 4c) show the same separation within one family. Middle-block rotation cost rose from 0.09 nats at k = 1 to 0.89 at k = 8, the six group means ordered exactly as k (exact permutation test over the 720 orderings, one-sided p = 1/720; mixed-effects slope 0.42 ± 0.06 nats per unit log k), and identity skipping gave the same ladder. The gain ratio stayed within 0.89–1.13 at every k, rising modestly with order (Spearman ρ = 0.60). Context length, dependence and difficulty change together with k, and two further source families separate them in part. In 30 models trained to copy the token k positions back (lags up to 32), skipping any block after the first two costs at most 0.05 nats, so distance alone does not recruit the middle layers. Thirty models were trained on random back-off Markov sources over eight tokens with the conditional entropy fixed at 1.00 nats at every order m ∈ {1, …, 6}. Middle-block rotation cost rose with order through m = 5 and fell slightly at m = 6, the order least fully learned (Spearman ρ = 0.83 over all 30 models, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.93; 0.87 over the 25 models within 0.025 nats of the source entropy, a split made after inspection), while the gain ratio was not associated with order (ρ = −0.22, CI −0.53 to 0.16; Figure 4d,e). This family holds entropy fixed but not the number of contexts, 8^m^. Position, confidence and entropy controls are in Supplemental notes 2 and 3.

Across the 50 matched-assay models the middle-block divergence of the assay and the rotation cost agree (ρ = 0.91), and middle-block divergence rises with the gain ratio (ρ = 0.80; Figure S8e) because the models with the lowest ratios, trained on shuffled text or low-order sources, have the least middle-layer dependence (Figure 4e); within the k-gram family, however, the dependence rises tenfold across an almost unchanged gain profile. Architecture also changes the gain profile (Figures S5 and S10): gates on the interior residual path deepen a middle valley and bypass routes flatten it, while middle-block rotation cost stays at 0.52–0.73 nats in every residual design. What perturbation sensitivity measures, against the decodability of task state and recovery by retraining, is examined in Figure S9 and Supplemental notes 1 and 4.

### Gain profiles and intervention sensitivity across 45 pretrained models

The 45 pretrained models span autoregressive decoders (GPT-2, Pythia, Qwen2.5, Llama-3.2, Mistral[@mistral], Gemma-2, Phi-2[@phi2]), state-space models (Mamba[@mamba]), convolutional networks (ConvNeXt[@convnext]), MLP-Mixers, vision transformers (ViT[@vit]; DINOv2[@dinov2]), masked encoders (BERT[@devlin]; RoBERTa[@roberta]), T5 encoders[@raffel] and audio encoders, from 28M to 14.7B parameters (Figure 5a; Table S1). The middle-to-edge gain ratio varies continuously across them (0.22–1.34; Hartigan dip test[@hartigan] p = 0.71), and 14 models in five architecture classes have an interior valley. Profiling 11 of the models with 30 inputs instead of five changed the ratio by at most 0.08 and no interior-valley classification, although the five-input intervals were too narrow in three models (Experimental procedures).

A census of the same models rotates the branch outputs of one block at a time and reads out each model's own objective on natural inputs (Figure 5b; Figures S6 and S7; Table S2). Across the 44 models with an interpretable readout (Flan-T5-large excluded by a pre-set criterion; Experimental procedures), the census shows no clear association between relative middle-block sensitivity and the gain ratio (Spearman ρ = 0.05, 95% CI −0.25 to 0.36; 0.04 with the 30-input ratios), or among the 23 decoders alone (ρ = −0.18). The interval does not exclude a moderate association, and the census is descriptive, since architecture, objective, data, scale and readout all vary across it.

### Controlled and pretrained models compared

Table 2 sets the two panels side by side. The relation between gain and consequence per unit squared displacement holds in both, with pooled slopes of 2.01 and 2.12 and within-model correlations positive in 44 of 50 and five of six models. The panels differ in three respects that matter for practice. Activation norms grow 1.2- to 104-fold over the interior blocks of the controlled models and 3- to 12-fold in the decoders; under the relative-dose convention the within-model correlation is negative in every shuffled-text and random-label model (norm growth 10.6- to 104-fold) and in four of the six decoders, but the pooled slope reverses sign only in the controlled panel (−0.61 against 1.89 in the decoders). The map after a block amplifies the leading local direction in the controlled models (downstream factor 2.2 to 13) and attenuates it slightly in five of the six decoders (0.57 to 0.83). Held-out prediction from gain is stronger in the controlled panel (R^2^ 0.56 against 0.24 with a model held out), and the exceptions differ: in the controlled panel the k = 1 models and the held-out k-gram orders, in the decoders Pythia-410M and Pythia-1.4B. A method validated on small controlled transformers should therefore not be assumed to carry its downstream behaviour to pretrained decoders, and the reverse.

## Discussion

Local gain is informative about the consequence of a perturbation per unit squared displacement. In 50 controlled transformers and six pretrained decoders from five families, normalised sensitivity rose with σ~1~ at a pooled log–log slope near 2, and gain improved the held-out prediction of a model's sensitivity profile beyond depth, activation norm and random-direction gain: beyond each separately for held-out models, text conditions and pretrained families, and beyond the three as a joint baseline for held-out models (paired difference 0.14, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.21) and, at the margin of its interval, for held-out pretrained families (0.22, −0.01 to 0.42). Sizing the perturbation as a fraction of the activation norm adds a factor ‖h‖^2^ that grows with depth; where that growth is large, in the shuffled-text and random-label models, the two conventions rank blocks in opposite orders, and in the pretrained decoders, where it is 3- to 12-fold, they diverge less. A ranking of layers by gain can therefore be interpreted only with the perturbation convention stated, and a perturbation study that reports a relative dose is, in part, reporting the norm profile.

The prediction has limits, and they are part of the result. Depth learned from other Markov orders already captures the profile of a held-out order, so gain adds nothing there; Pythia-410M's profile runs against its gain and no predictor set beats a flat profile for Pythia-1.4B; and where the norm coefficient does not transfer, as between the text and k-gram families, adding gain to a baseline that contains the norm does not repair it. The leading local direction is more consequential than random directions in every model tested, but the source of the excess differs: in the pretrained decoders it is local gain alone, and the map after the block attenuates the leading direction slightly, whereas in the small controlled models that map amplifies it further, most at k = 1, and this downstream factor is not consistently associated with local gain across blocks. Because the downstream factor of Equation 2 does not depend on the logit coordinates, this conclusion does not rest on the split into amplification and alignment, which does; the split with the logit mean removed agrees with it. Whether the later blocks or the output head carry the amplification in the controlled models is not resolved here.

Sensitivity to a small displacement at one token and dependence on a whole block are different quantities, and only the first follows gain. Middle-layer intervention sensitivity follows the dependency structure of the training data: it rises tenfold with order in fitted k-gram sources and rises with order at fixed conditional entropy, while the gain profile changes little in these families. Gain profiles change during training in other settings (Figure S4), and an interior valley forms in 14 of 45 pretrained models and in particular architectural designs; in neither setting does the profile mark the layers on which the network depends. Gain is a local property of a block's map, and which blocks a network depends on is set by what the data require of the network, which need not show in that map.

### Limitations of the study

The controlled results rest on 12-layer transformers of 63–89M parameters, with the central contrast and the matched assay repeated at 24 layers and the assay in six pretrained decoders of 124M to 2.6B parameters; larger decoders were surveyed and censused but not assayed. Pretrained survey profiles rest on five inputs per model (30 for 11 of them), whose intervals understate the sampling spread. Normalised sensitivity is the measured divergence divided by the squared displacement within the quadratic regime, not a separate fixed-absolute intervention; centred R^2^ measures the shape of a profile, not its level; the linearised decomposition was run on eight controlled checkpoints and the six pretrained decoders; branch rotation measures sensitivity to disruption of the learned branch-to-stream relationship, not indispensability; and the census compares families under different readouts and is descriptive. The held-out prediction is a check of transfer within the populations studied, not an evaluation on new populations.

### Guidance for perturbation studies

Three practices follow from the results.

*Choosing the perturbation size.* Report the consequence per unit squared displacement within the quadratic regime, after checking that the divergence scales as ε^2^ over the range used (here up to ε = 0.3 of the input norm), and state the convention. If a relative dose is used, report the activation-norm profile beside the ranking, because the relative-dose consequence carries a factor ‖h~l~‖^2^, and ‖h~l~‖ itself grew 1.2- to 104-fold over the compared blocks here. In the models here the two conventions disagreed where norm growth over the compared blocks exceeded tenfold (the shuffled-text and random-label models) and diverged less where it was a few-fold (the pretrained decoders), so the norm profile says in advance how much the choice will matter. Reporting both conventions costs one division per probe.

*Interpreting gain.* State the operator (J^T^J, not J), the precision (float32) and the probe context (the token in its natural context), because each changes the value (Table 1). Read σ~1~ as the amplification of a small displacement at one token; a value near 1 does not mean an inactive branch. Expect it to predict the shape of a model's normalised-sensitivity profile beyond depth, norm and random-direction gain when the model resembles the population the coefficients were fitted on, and expect the failures seen here: profiles that run against gain, families whose sensitivity depth alone captures, and baselines whose norm term does not transfer. Do not read a gain profile, or an interior valley in one, as a map of the blocks a network depends on.

*Checking downstream consequences.* The consequence of a direction is its squared gain times a downstream factor. Compute the downstream factor of Equation 2 from one forward-mode product per direction, which does not depend on the logit coordinates, compare the leading direction with norm-matched random directions, and verify the second-order prediction against a measured divergence at small ε before relying on it. A claim about whole-block dependence needs a whole-block intervention, such as branch rotation, identity skipping or activation patching[@meng; @heimersheim], read out on the model's own objective; gain geometry yields hypotheses that such interventions can test, and it does not replace them.

### Local gain estimator

For a target block l, a forward pre-hook captures the block's input activations on a natural forward pass and every keyword argument the block receives (attention masks, position embeddings, cache state), and the block is then called directly as a function of one token's hidden state. The local function f: ℝ^d^ → ℝ^d^ maps the hidden state of token t before the block to the hidden state of the same token after it, with all other positions held fixed at their natural values; J = ∂h~l+1,t~/∂h~l,t~ is a d × d matrix and σ~1~(J) its leading singular value. Each iteration is v ← J^T^(Jv)/‖J^T^(Jv)‖, with the forward product Jv computed by forward-mode automatic differentiation (torch.func.jvp) and the adjoint product J^T^w by reverse-mode autograd on the same hooked call; σ~1~ is read as ‖Jv‖ at convergence, when the relative change falls below 10^−6^. No Jacobian is materialised and no gradient is propagated through the rest of the network. Each estimate uses 50 power-iteration steps from four random starts, batched, with the maximum over starts reported; the value at a block is the mean over five inputs and eight token positions spaced from T/8 to T − 1 (40 samples per block), and the per-sample values are retained for the bootstrap. TF32 matrix multiplication was disabled and all models were loaded with eager attention, because scaled dot-product attention kernels do not support forward-mode differentiation. For convolutional models the token is a spatial position of the feature map and the eight positions form an interior grid. Mamba models used the reference PyTorch selective-scan implementation, which forward-mode differentiation handles exactly. One script (survey_sigma1_v2.py) produced every pretrained-model σ~1~ value in this paper; its plumbing test reproduces the singular value decomposition of materialised Jacobians to a relative error of about 10^−7^ on small randomly initialised instances of all 17 architectures profiled. Every profile in this paper is a token-local J^T^J power iteration in float32; the three places where the implementation differs (the width × depth grid and two training trajectories, profiled by their training scripts at a single position) are noted in Table S7 and in the legends concerned, and every result first obtained under an earlier estimator, with what replaced it, is set out in Supplemental note 5.

### Estimator validation

The token-local Jacobian was materialised (torch.func.jacrev) and decomposed exactly at five blocks spread over the depth, three positions and two natural inputs, in GPT-2 124M, Pythia-160M, Pythia-410M and Phi-2 (WikiText-103 inputs; 120 pairs; d between 768 and 2,560) and under the same protocol in ConvNeXt-tiny, ConvNeXt-base and Mamba-130M (CIFAR-100 and WikiText-103 inputs; 90 pairs; d = 96–1,024 for ConvNeXt, where the token is a spatial position and d the channel count, and 768 for Mamba). The values reported are from a rerun of this validation on 25 September 2026 (survey_sigma1_v2 v2.3, --validate, A100-40GB) with every per-pair value written to persistent storage; the original runs survive as three-decimal printed logs, which the rerun reproduces pair by pair to a maximum relative difference of 1.9 × 10^−4^, the precision of the logs. Relative error of the σ~1~ estimate: transformers median 7.5 × 10^−5^, maximum 3.0 × 10^−4^; ConvNeXt-tiny median 1.8 × 10^−5^, maximum 9.4 × 10^−5^; ConvNeXt-base 2.3 × 10^−5^ and 1.2 × 10^−4^; Mamba-130M 5.3 × 10^−5^ and 1.2 × 10^−4^; Pearson r = 1.000 in every model. ### Gain profiles

R~ex0~ = mean(σ~1~ over the middle third of blocks)/mean(σ~1~ over the two edge thirds), with blocks 1 to L − 1 divided into thirds and any remainder assigned to the middle; block 0 is excluded because its σ~1~ (35–195 in the Pythia, GPT-2 and Qwen decoders, against 2.3–19 for every other block) dominates the edge mean. A profile has an interior valley if R~ex0~ < 0.80 and both edge thirds exceed the middle third; the continuous ratio is primary and the classification secondary. A 95% confidence interval on R~ex0~ was obtained by resampling the five inputs as clusters, each carrying its eight positions across all blocks (2,000 resamples). The 11 models of Figures 2, 3 and 5 were profiled again with 30 inputs: the ratio moved by at most 0.08 (median 0.02), no classification changed, and the 30-input value lay outside the five-input interval in three models (Pythia-1.4B, ConvNeXt-base and Mamba-130M), so five-input intervals understate the sampling spread. Sweeping the valley threshold from 0.60 to 0.95 gives 6 to 22 interior-valley models among the 45 (14 at 0.80). Region lists for every measurement, and the agreement between the survey ratio and ratios matched to the intervened blocks, are in the supplemental methods.

Three exclusion criteria set before the analysis (a non-negative mean ΔL over the interior blocks, a defined ratio, and a cross-entropy readout below the uniform-prediction baseline ln V) exclude one model, Flan-T5-large, whose clean span-corruption loss lies above that baseline. The census statistic is the Spearman correlation between R~ex0~ and the relative interior sensitivity across the 44 remaining models, with a 95% percentile bootstrap interval over models (5,000 resamples), repeated on the 23 decoders alone and after leaving out each architecture family in turn. Gemma-2-2B alone was run with a beginning-of-sequence token prepended to each sequence, because without one its clean loss is 6.84 nats against 2.97 with one; the same change applied to Llama-3.2-1B leaves its ratio unchanged (supplemental methods). Eleven public Pythia-410M checkpoints (steps 0 to 143,000) were profiled with the survey estimator on 30 inputs and with the census rotation (Figure 5c; Figure S4c,d).

### Controlled models

*Training conditions.* Matched 12-layer pre-norm transformers (d = 512, eight heads, feed-forward width 2,048, untied 50,257-token prediction head, 89.4M parameters) were trained for 10,000 steps at sequence length 128, batch size 16, AdamW, learning rate 3 × 10^−4^, weight decay 0.01, 500 warm-up steps and gradient clipping at 1.0 under four conditions: causal training on WikiText-103; the same text with tokens permuted once within each sequence; real text with each target replaced by a uniformly random token resampled every batch; and bidirectional masked training on real text (15% of positions selected, of which 80% replaced by the end-of-text token, 10% by a random token and 10% left unchanged, with the loss on the selected positions). Five seeds per condition. Gain was profiled with the canonical estimator (five inputs × eight positions) at steps 1,000, 3,000, 5,000 and 10,000, and checkpoints were saved at 10,000 steps. Interior sensitivity was measured on the saved checkpoints by branch rotation at doses 0.25, 0.5 and 1 and by identity skipping over 50 batches of 16 sequences, read out under each model's own objective and, for the random-label models, on real text. The real-text and shuffled-text conditions were repeated with 24 blocks (127.2M parameters), three seeds each, with the region lists rescaled to thirds of 24, and the six checkpoints were then probed with the matched assay. Condition switching (5,000 steps under one condition and 5,000 under another, both directions between shuffled and real text, three seeds per direction), the mask × loss design, the width × depth grid, the routing-topology and skip-coefficient sweeps and the bracket-nesting probes are described in the supplemental methods.

*Dependency-order sweep.* Thirty transformers (63.6M parameters; feed-forward width 2,048, tied head, dropout 0.1) were trained on k-gram Markov sources fitted to a 20,000-sequence WikiText-103 corpus (vocabulary 50,257; for each context of k − 1 tokens the 200 most frequent successors with their empirical probabilities, backing off to shorter contexts and finally to the unigram distribution), k ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8}, five seeds per value, a fixed corpus of 20,000 generated sequences of length 128 per k and seed, 10,000 steps of AdamW (learning rate 3 × 10^−4^, weight decay 0.01, 500 warm-up steps then cosine decay). The models were profiled with the canonical estimator on five held-out sequences from their own source at initialisation, step 5,000 and step 10,000, and sensitivity was measured on 800 held-out sequences from the same source: branch rotation and identity skipping of the early (0–3), middle (4–7) and late (8–11) blocks, per-block rotation and skipping, and early-exit loss (Figure S11). Conditional entropy of each source was computed from the empirical tables; the entropy-perturbation control (k ∈ {2, 5, 8} × uniform-noise mixing ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9} × five seeds) and the position-corrected per-block maps are in Supplemental notes 2 and 3.

*Entropy-matched Markov family.* Thirty models with the k-gram architecture, optimiser, schedule and measurements were trained on random back-off Markov sources over a vocabulary of eight tokens: the transition logits of a context are the sum over r = 1..m of independent standard-normal tables indexed by the last r tokens, so every order contributes and the order-m table is needed to reach the floor, and the exact conditional entropy H(X~t~ | last m) under the stationary law is set to 1.000 nats by a temperature found by bisection, with low-order tables redrawn until the marginal entropy lies within 0.1 nats of ln 8. Orders m ∈ {1, …, 6} (8 to 262,144 contexts), five seeds each, fresh sequences every batch, 800 held-out sequences. The learned gap between the model's loss and the source floor was 0.000 (m = 1 and 2), 0.001, 0.007, 0.025 and 0.072 nats (m = 6, whose sixth token is used but whose table is not fully learned), and every model beats the best order-(m − 1) predictor. The 0.025-nat criterion that separates orders 1–5 from order 6 was set after inspecting these gaps, so the ordering statistics are reported for the 25 models within it and for all 30.

*Lag family.* Sixty 12-layer transformers (d = 512, 42M parameters over a 4,096-token vocabulary) were trained for 10,000 steps on synthetic sources with fresh data every batch. In the lag family the token at position t equals the token at t − k with probability 0.8 and a uniform token otherwise; in the pair family it equals, with the same probability, the sum modulo 4,096 of the tokens at t − 1 and t − k; k ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32}, five seeds each. Each model was profiled with the canonical estimator and probed by identity skipping of each block on 50 held-out batches. The pair family reached the ceiling in every run and is reported only as a failure to learn.

### Interventions

Branch rotation multiplies, for a selected block with residual update h~l+1~ = h~l~ + A~l~(h~l~) + F~l~(h~l~ + A~l~(h~l~)), the attention output A~l~ and the feed-forward output F~l~ each by a random orthogonal matrix Q drawn from the Haar measure on O(d), one matrix per branch, before the residual add. This preserves the norm of each branch output and disrupts the learned alignment between the branch and the residual stream; it does not in general preserve the norm of the resulting residual state, so equal rotation dose is not equal perturbation magnitude across blocks, and the per-block magnitude is reported. Sensitivity is ΔL = L~perturbed~ − L~clean~ in nats. The consequence of branch rotation measures the network's sensitivity to that disruption, not the indispensability of the block's computation, and no information-preservation claim is made for the full residual state. Identity skipping, which replaces a block's output by its input, removes the computation while preserving alignment and is the more direct test of necessity. An interpolation parameter with QR re-orthogonalisation gave low-dose variants at 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75. Construct validity against resample, mean and zero ablation, the downstream-only adaptation test, and the block-by-block comparison of branch rotation with whole-block rotation and identity skipping on eight pretrained models (whole-block rotation saturates at 8–10 nats at every block in every decoder and cannot rank blocks; branch rotation and identity skipping agree at Spearman 0.59–0.96 over interior blocks in seven of eight models) are in Supplemental note 4 and the supplemental methods.

### Matched assay

Each of the 50 controlled checkpoints was probed on five held-out sequences of its own training distribution at positions 16, 48, 80 and 112. For every block the top right singular vector v~1~ of the token-local Jacobian at that position was obtained by the J^T^J power iteration (50 iterations, two restarts, tolerance 10^−6^), and the block input at that position was displaced by ±ε‖h‖u with u = v~1~ or one of four random unit vectors, ε ∈ {0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1}, signs averaged, in a single batched forward pass. Four readouts were recorded: the gain ‖Δ(block output at the position)‖/‖δ‖, which equals σ~1~ along v~1~ as ε → 0 (median ratio 0.999 over 600 block–model pairs, 5th to 95th percentile 0.994–1.000); the Kullback–Leibler divergence of the output distribution at the position (D~t~); the mean divergence over the positions the perturbation can reach (D̄; the position onward under a causal mask, every position under the bidirectional mask); and the change in the model's own loss over the same positions. The divergence readouts scale as ε^2^ up to ε = 0.3 and begin to saturate at ε = 1, so ε = 0.1 is reported. Normalised sensitivity S~l~ = D/‖δ~l~‖^2^ with ‖δ~l~‖ = ε‖h~l,t~‖ recorded per probe, averaged over the 20 probes of each block; the same ratio is what a fixed absolute displacement would give, since the divergence scales as ε^2^ in this range. Under the relative-dose convention the within-model Spearman correlation over blocks 1–10 has a median of −0.02 (25 of 50 positive; real text −0.45 to 0.31, shuffled −0.93 to −0.10, random labels −0.95 to −0.75, bidirectional 0.12 to 0.82; k = 1, −1.00 to −0.25; k = 4 and 5, 0.38 to 0.83; k = 8, −0.35 to 0.33); under the absolute convention it has a median of 0.60 (44 of 50 positive; real text 0.72–0.85, shuffled 0.59–0.96, random labels 0.32–0.98, bidirectional 0.58–0.93; k = 1, −0.96 to −0.02; k = 2 and 3, 0.18–0.98; k = 4 to 8, −0.47 to 0.85), and the two sets of per-model values are unrelated (Spearman 0.08). A repeat of the assay on ten checkpoints with 20 inputs × six positions and eight random directions per probe moved single-model correlations by up to a few tenths while the pattern across conditions was unchanged (supplemental methods). The whole assay took 28 min on one GPU and no model was retrained.

GPT-2 124M, Pythia-410M, Pythia-1.4B, Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen2.5-1.5B and Gemma-2-2B (a beginning-of-sequence token prepended for Gemma-2-2B, as in the census) were probed with the same assay and the linearised response in one run (float32, eager attention, TF32 disabled; one NVIDIA A100 40 GB, 8–198 min per model). Inputs were 20 WikiText-103 validation sequences of 128 tokens spread evenly through the split, at positions t ∈ {8, 32, 56, 80, 104, 120}, for every block, with eight random unit vectors drawn once per input and position and ε ∈ {0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3}. Within-model correlations are over blocks 1 to L − 2, with 95% intervals from 500 resamples of whole inputs; pooled regressions express depth as block/(L − 1) because L differs between models, with standard errors clustered on the six models. At ε = 0.01 a minority of probes in GPT-2, Qwen2.5 and Gemma-2 gave random-direction divergences near the float32 resolution of the log-softmax over their large vocabularies, so ratios of measured divergences are summarised at ε = 0.1, as in the controlled models, and the decomposition factors use the gains at ε = 0.01 with the analytic linearised response.

### Downstream linearised response

For a probe (block l, position t) and a unit direction u at the block input, the same-position divergence is, to second order, D~KL~ ≈ ½ε^2^‖h~l,t~‖^2^ g^T^Fg with g = Au, where A is the Jacobian of the position-t logits with respect to the block-l input at t (the whole downstream stack, block l included) and F = diag(p) − pp^T^; g is one forward-mode product through the model. Dividing ‖g‖^2^ by the squared local gain along the same direction (σ~1~ for v~1~, and the assay's own random-direction gain at ε = 0.01 for the random class, the two runs having drawn the same directions from the same generator) leaves the amplification contributed by the blocks after block l, so the same-position consequence ratio factorises as (σ~1~/g~rand~)^2^ × after-block amplification × alignment. The downstream factor g^T^Fg/‖Ju‖^2^ is unchanged when a constant is added to every logit. Per model it is the geometric mean, over probes within a block and then over blocks (1–10 controlled; 1 to L − 2 pretrained), of the per-probe ratio of v~1~ to the mean over the random directions, with 95% percentile intervals from 500 resamples of probes within blocks (controlled, 20 per block) or of inputs (pretrained, five). The response was computed on the eight controlled checkpoints named in the Results (five inputs × four positions, v~1~ and four random directions per probe) and on the first five inputs of each pretrained decoder (v~1~ and the same eight random directions). The amplification–alignment split is reported in the mean-centred gauge, ‖g − mean(g)1‖^2^, from a rerun that also records mean(g) per direction and reproduces σ~1~, ‖g‖^2^ and g^T^Fg to a median relative deviation below 10^−6^; the raw-coordinate values, and the constant component of ‖g‖^2^ in each model, are in the supplemental methods.

### Statistics

Within each model the relation between σ~1~ and consequence is summarised by the Spearman correlation over blocks 1–10 (1 to L − 2 in the pretrained decoders), under each convention, and also with block position partialled out and over first differences (Table S3). Across models, log consequence is regressed on log σ~1~ with a fixed intercept per model and block position as a linear covariate (50 models × blocks 1–10, 500 rows), with cluster-robust standard errors (the trained model as the cluster) and percentile intervals from 2,000 resamples of models; two sensitivity specifications replace the linear term by a separate effect per block or add a linear depth trend per model, and condition-specific fits are given in Table S3. Held-out prediction compares six predictor sets on identical folds: block position (depth); depth with the log activation norm ‖h~l~‖; depth with the log gain along a random unit direction at ε = 0.01, which equals the Frobenius gain up to a constant that centring removes; depth with log σ~1~; the joint baseline of depth with the norm and the random-direction gain; and the joint baseline with log σ~1~. Within a model the three block-level predictors are correlated (median |r| 0.69–0.82 with one another and 0.55–0.99 with block position). For each fold the coefficients of a set, with a fixed intercept per model, are fitted on the training models and the within-model-centred log profile of each held-out model over blocks 1–10 is predicted from the non-model terms, with R^2^ = 1 − ΣSS~res~/ΣSS~tot~ pooled over the held-out models. The folds hold out one model (50), one text condition (4), one k-gram order (6), the 30 k-gram models (fitted on the 20 text models) or the 20 text models; in the pretrained decoders one model (6) or one family (5, the two Pythia models together), with depth as block/(L − 1). A difference between two sets on the same folds (depth with gain against depth with the random-direction gain; the joint baseline with gain against without) is given a percentile interval from 2,000 resamples of the held-out models, and each pretrained fold's R^2^ a 95% interval from 500 resamples of the held-out model's inputs with the training fit held fixed. A coefficient per block in place of the linear depth term changes the σ~1~ set by at most 0.04. These analyses ask what gain adds beyond position within the populations studied; they do not replace the condition-specific results, which are the unit at which the pattern is reported.

The primary test for the dependency-order effect used the six k-group means as inferential units and an exact permutation test over the 720 orderings of six groups, with the directional hypothesis (ΔL~middle~ increasing in k) specified before the experiment; the observed ordering is the one extreme ordering, p = 1/720 (one-sided). A mixed-effects model with a random intercept per k level was fitted as a co-primary confirmation (slope 0.42 ± 0.06 nats per unit log k); with six levels the variance of the random intercept is weakly identified, so the permutation test is primary. The same test over the five learned orders of the entropy-matched family gives p = 1/120. Other comparisons report mean ± s.d. across seeds and Spearman or Pearson correlations as stated, with percentile bootstrap intervals over models, inputs or probes as stated in each legend; uncertainty is reported at three levels, across inputs, across intervention draws and across training seeds, and the unit of the condition contrasts is the training seed. All evaluation passes use model.eval() with dropout off, fixed masks and fixed random seeds.
